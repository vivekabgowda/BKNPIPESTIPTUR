NP2 vs NP3 RCC Pipes: Which Class Do You Need?

The difference between NP2 and NP3 pipes decides whether your culvert lasts 50 years or cracks in the first monsoon. Here is a plain-language guide to IS 458 pipe classes for contractors and panchayat engineers.

Last updated: July 2026

Walk into any tender meeting in Karnataka and you will hear the same question: "NP2 kodona, NP3 bekaa?" — will NP2 do, or do we need NP3? The two classes look identical stacked in a yard, but they differ in wall thickness, reinforcement steel and, most importantly, the crushing load they must withstand under IS 458. Choose too low a class and the pipe fails under traffic; choose too high and you overpay on every metre of the line. This guide explains the difference between NP2 and NP3 pipes, when each is the right choice, and how to write the class correctly into your BOQ.

What the NP Classes Mean Under IS 458

IS 458:2021 (Precast Concrete Pipes — with and without reinforcement) is the Indian Standard that governs RCC pipes. It divides non-pressure pipes — the "NP" series, designed for gravity flow of storm water, sewage and irrigation water — into four classes based on duty, i.e., the external load the pipe is designed to carry. There is also a separate "P" series (P1, P2, P3) for pressure pipes that are hydrostatically tested, but for drains and culverts the NP series is what matters.

ClassReinforcementDuty levelTypical use per IS 458
NP1UnreinforcedVery lightDrainage and irrigation, above ground or in shallow trenches
NP2ReinforcedLight dutyDrainage and irrigation; cross drains and culverts carrying light traffic
NP3ReinforcedMedium dutyDrainage and irrigation; cross drains and culverts carrying medium traffic
NP4ReinforcedHeavy dutyCulverts and cross drains carrying heavy traffic, e.g., national highways

A point worth stressing, because loose market talk gets it wrong constantly: NP3 is the medium-duty class, not the heavy-duty class. Heavy-duty is NP4, which IS 458 reserves for crossings under heavy traffic such as national highways. For the vast majority of rural and district-level work — field drains, village culverts, panchayat road crossings, minor irrigation lines — the practical choice is between NP2 and NP3, which is exactly why BKN Group manufactures these two classes at our Kunigal and Tiptur units, as per IS 458 specifications. For a fuller walkthrough of the standard itself, see our guide on IS 458 explained in plain language.

The Real Difference: Load-Bearing Strength

The class of a pipe is defined by how much load it can take before cracking. IS 458 measures this through the three-edge bearing test: a sample pipe is laid on two bottom bearing strips and a load is applied in a line along the crown (top) of the pipe. The load is increased until the pipe shows a crack of specified width, and then further until ultimate failure. Each class and diameter has its own required crack load and ultimate load laid down in the standard — the higher the class, the higher the load the pipe must survive. (Per the BIS product manual, three-edge bearing and permeability tests are carried out on sampled control units — for example every 10th control unit for sizes up to and including 600 mm.)

To meet that higher load, an NP3 pipe of the same diameter carries more reinforcement steel and a thicker barrel wall than an NP2 pipe. As broad ranges, NP2 barrel walls run from roughly 25 mm on the smallest diameters up to about 110 mm at the top of the NP2 range, while NP3 walls run from roughly 25 mm up to about 215 mm — NP3 is also produced in larger diameters than NP2, and above roughly 900 mm diameter, NP3 (or NP4) is generally preferred over NP2. The exact wall thickness, reinforcement and test-load values for each diameter are as per IS 458 tables — a detailed spec sheet is available from us on request.

In practice, the difference shows up when a loaded tipper or tractor-trailer passes over a buried pipe. The wheel load travels down through the fill and squeezes the pipe barrel. A light-duty NP2 pipe under a farm drain sees very little of this; the same pipe placed under a busy district road sees it hundreds of times a day, and cracks. That is the entire NP2 vs NP3 question in one sentence: how much traffic and fill will sit above the pipe?

Because NP3 uses more steel and a thicker wall, it costs more per pipe. Seen against the total cost of a culvert — excavation, bedding, headwalls, jointing, backfilling — the class upgrade is usually a small fraction of the job. Replacing a cracked pipe under a live road, on the other hand, means re-excavating the entire crossing and blocking traffic. Saving a few thousand rupees on class is rarely worth it where traffic is involved.

Common Mistakes We See on Site

  • Using NP2 under trafficked roads. The most frequent and most expensive mistake. An NP2 pipe placed under a road with regular truck movement is loaded beyond its class every single day. Cracks may not show immediately — they show in the first heavy monsoon, when the road above settles.
  • Calling NP3 "heavy duty". Market shorthand often labels NP3 as heavy duty. Under IS 458 it is medium duty; NP4 is the heavy-duty class. If a design drawing for a highway crossing says "heavy duty RCC pipe", clarify the class in writing before ordering.
  • Ignoring cover depth. Traffic is not the only load — deep earth fill also crushes pipes. A drain with 4 m of fill above it needs more strength than the same drain at 1 m, even with zero traffic.
  • Writing only "RCC hume pipe" in the BOQ. Without the class stated, the lowest-priced (lowest-class) pipe wins the comparison, and the site inherits the problem.
  • Mixing classes in one crossing. Topping up a short culvert with whatever pipes are lying in the yard means the weakest pipe sets the strength of the whole crossing.

How to Specify NP2/NP3 in a BOQ or Tender

A pipe line item should pin down five things: class, diameter, effective length, standard, and joint type. A sample BOQ description:

"Providing and supplying RCC spun (centrifugally cast) pipes, Class NP3, 600 mm internal diameter, 2.5 m effective length, manufactured as per IS 458:2021, with collar joints including precast concrete collars, jointed with cement mortar, including loading, unloading and stacking at site — per metre / per pipe."

Notes on each element:

  • Class: NP2 or NP3 as per the decision guide above — never leave it blank.
  • Effective length: standard lengths are 2.0 m and 2.5 m (IS 458 permits longer lengths up to 4 m for larger diameters). Specify it, because rates quoted "per pipe" mean nothing without the length.
  • Joint type: IS 458 covers spigot & socket joints (rubber-ring, flexible), collar joints (mortar or bitumen-mastic filled) and flush joints (tongue-and-groove, smooth bore). For road culverts and cross drains, collar-jointed and flush-jointed pipes are the usual practice; rubber-ring flexible joints are required for pressure lines and most other non-pressure work.
  • Wall thickness and reinforcement: do not copy numbers from random catalogues — state "as per IS 458 tables" and ask the supplier for the spec sheet.
  • Testing: IS 458 acceptance tests include the three-edge bearing test, hydrostatic test, permeability test and water absorption test. Your tender can reference these tests as per the standard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use NP2 pipes under a road?

Only where the crossing carries light traffic — a farm approach or an internal village lane. For cross drains and culverts under medium traffic, IS 458 assigns NP3, and for heavy traffic such as national highways it assigns NP4. Using NP2 under a regularly trafficked road is the single most common cause of premature culvert failure we hear about.

Is NP3 a heavy-duty pipe?

No. Under IS 458:2021, NP3 is the medium-duty non-pressure class. The heavy-duty class is NP4, meant for heavy-traffic crossings like national highways. Treat "heavy duty" on a quotation with caution and always confirm the actual class in writing.
