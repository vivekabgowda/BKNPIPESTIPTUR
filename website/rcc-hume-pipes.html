NP2 vs NP3 — Which Class Do You Need?

Under IS 458, "NP" means non-pressure: these pipes carry water by gravity flow (drains, sewers, culverts), not pumped pressure. The number indicates the duty level:

  • NP2 — light duty, reinforced. For drainage and irrigation lines, and for cross drains and culverts carrying light traffic. The economical choice for most gram panchayat drain works and farm crossings.
  • NP3 — medium duty, reinforced. For drainage and irrigation where the pipe sits under medium traffic or deeper earth cover — typically culverts on busier village, taluk and district roads.

For completeness: IS 458 also defines NP1 (unreinforced, light drainage use) and NP4 (heavy duty, for culverts under heavy traffic such as national highways), plus P1/P2/P3 pressure classes. BKN manufactures the NP2 and NP3 classes, which cover the large majority of drainage, irrigation and culvert work in our region.

The practical rule of thumb: if vehicles will pass over the pipe regularly, or the trench is deep, step up to NP3; if it is a plain drain or a light-traffic crossing with normal cover, NP2 usually serves well at a lower cost. Manufactured as per IS 458:2021

Our pipes are manufactured as per IS 458:2021 — Precast Concrete Pipes (with and without reinforcement), the Indian Standard that governs RCC pipe manufacture. The standard specifies, for each diameter and class, the concrete strength, barrel wall thickness, reinforcement (longitudinal and spiral), permissible dimensional tolerances, joint details and the test loads the pipe must withstand. When a tender says "RCC NP3 pipes conforming to IS 458", these tables are what it refers to.

What the IS 458 test regime covers

It helps buyers to know what the standard's quality and acceptance tests actually check:

  • Three-edge bearing test — a line load is applied along the pipe crown until the specified crack load and ultimate load; this is the test that establishes a pipe's strength class (NP2, NP3 and so on). Load values are as per IS 458 for each diameter and class.
  • Hydrostatic test — internal water pressure is held for a specified period to check that the pipe wall does not leak.
  • Permeability test — checks how much water passes through the concrete wall, a measure of the concrete's density.
  • Water absorption test — cut samples are weighed dry and soaked to confirm the concrete's imperviousness.

Under the standard's sampling scheme, tests such as three-edge bearing and permeability are carried out on sampled control units rather than on every single pipe — for example, on every 10th control unit for sizes up to and including 600 mm.

BKN's in-house quality control — stated plainly

We believe in telling buyers exactly what we do. BKN runs a strict in-house quality control routine on every batch:

Joint Types: Spigot & Socket vs Collar

IS 458 provides for three joint types on concrete pipes, and the right one depends on what the line carries and what passes over it:

Joint typeHow it worksWhere it is used
Spigot & socketOne end is plain (spigot), the other is enlarged (socket); a rubber ring seals the joint, giving a flexible, watertight connection.Required for pressure lines, and preferred for sewage and other non-pressure lines where leak-tightness matters and minor ground settlement must be absorbed — but not for road culverts/cross drains.
Collar jointBoth pipe ends are plain; a separate precast concrete collar is placed over the junction and the annular gap is filled with cement mortar or bitumen mastic.The common choice for culverts, cross drains and general stormwater/irrigation lines — simple to lay and repair with basic site labour.
Flush jointTongue on one end, groove on the other, sealed with mortar, mastic or a rubber ring — giving a smooth internal surface across the joint.Used on culvert and cross-drain work where a flush internal bore is wanted.

In practice: for a road culvert under a village or district road, collar-jointed pipes are the standard, economical answer. For a sewage line where infiltration and leakage must be controlled, a flexible rubber-ring joint is the better engineering choice. Tell us the application when you enquire and we will confirm the joint arrangement, collar requirement and jointing material for your order. Collars are charged separately from the pipe rate.

How Our Pipes Are Made: The Spun (Centrifugal) Process

All BKN pipes are spun pipes — made by the centrifugal casting method that gives hume pipes their name and their strength. In brief:

  1. A welded reinforcement cage (longitudinal bars plus spiral winding) is fabricated and placed inside a steel mould;
  2. Measured concrete is fed into the mould as it rotates at high speed on the spinning machine;
  3. Centrifugal force drives the concrete outward, compacting it densely against the mould wall and throwing excess water to the centre — producing a dense, low-porosity wall with a smooth internal finish;
  4. The pipe is demoulded and then water-cured for the full curing period before inspection and stacking.

The dense, compacted wall is why a properly spun and cured RCC pipe resists water absorption and gives decades of buried service life. For the full step-by-step process — moulds, cages, spinning, curing and inspection — read how RCC spun pipes are manufactured.

Applications

  • Stormwater drainage — roadside and trunk storm drains in towns, layouts and villages; 300–900 mm are the workhorse sizes.
  • Sewage lines — gravity sewers and outfalls; RCC handles continuous wet service that corrodes metal pipe.
  • Culverts and cross drains — under panchayat, taluk and district roads; class chosen to suit traffic (NP2 for light, NP3 for medium traffic).
  • Irrigation — canal outlets, field-channel crossings and conveyance lines on farms and lift-irrigation schemes.
  • Road crossings — carrying drains or utility lines under existing roads with minimal disturbance.
  • Casing / sleeve pipes — large diameters (900–1600 mm) used as protective casings for cables and smaller service lines.
