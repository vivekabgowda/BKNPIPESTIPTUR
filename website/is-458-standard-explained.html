IS 458 Standard for Concrete Pipes, Explained in Plain English

IS 458 is the Indian Standard that governs precast concrete pipes — the rulebook behind every "Hume pipe" tender in the country. This guide explains its classes, tests and specifications in simple language so you can order the right pipe with confidence.

Last updated: July 2026

What is IS 458 and why does it matter to you?

IS 458 is the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) specification titled Precast Concrete Pipes (with and without reinforcement). The current edition is IS 458:2021, which superseded the older IS 458:2003. Almost every RCC pipe used in Indian drainage, irrigation and culvert work — whether you call it a Hume pipe, spun pipe or cement pipe — is made and bought with reference to this one document.

Why should a contractor or panchayat engineer care? Because IS 458 gives everyone in a transaction a common language. When a tender says "900 mm NP3 pipe as per IS 458", both the buyer and the manufacturer know exactly what concrete strength, reinforcement, wall thickness, length and quality checks are expected. Without the standard, "RCC pipe" could mean almost anything — and a pipe that looks fine in the yard could crack under the first loaded lorry that crosses the culvert.

You do not need to read the full standard to buy pipes correctly. You only need to understand four things: the pipe classes, what the standard specifies, the tests it defines, and how to write your order. That is exactly what this guide covers.

Scope of the IS 458 concrete pipe standard: the pipe classes

IS 458 divides precast concrete pipes into two families: pressure pipes (P classes), which carry water under internal pressure, and non-pressure pipes (NP classes), which carry water by gravity flow only — drains, culverts, irrigation channels and sewers. Each class is a step up in strength, and therefore in cost.

ClassTypeReinforcementTypical use
NP1Non-pressure, unreinforcedNoneDrainage and irrigation above ground or in shallow trenches
NP2Non-pressure, light-dutyReinforcedDrainage, irrigation, cross drains and culverts under light traffic
NP3Non-pressure, medium-dutyReinforcedDrainage, irrigation, cross drains and culverts under medium traffic
NP4Non-pressure, heavy-dutyReinforcedCulverts and cross drains under heavy traffic, e.g. national highways
P1, P2, P3Pressure pipesReinforcedPiped water supply under internal pressure; hydrostatically tested

A simple way to remember the NP ladder: the higher the number, the heavier the load the buried pipe can carry from soil and traffic above it. For a detailed comparison of the two most commonly ordered classes, see our guide on NP2 vs NP3 pipes.

What IS 458 actually specifies

The standard is not just a list of classes. It fixes rules for how a compliant pipe must be made, from raw material to finished surface.

Materials

IS 458 requires that the cement, aggregates, water and reinforcing steel used in the pipe conform to their own respective Indian Standards. In practice this means clean graded aggregates, potable-quality mixing water and cement of a specified type and grade — not whatever happens to be cheapest that week.

Reinforcement

For reinforced classes (NP2 upwards and all P classes), the standard lays down the quantity and arrangement of the steel cage inside the pipe wall — longitudinal bars plus a spiral — along with the minimum concrete cover that protects the steel from rusting. The reinforcement quantities vary with diameter and class, as per IS 458 tables.

Dimensions and tolerances

IS 458 fixes the internal diameter, barrel wall thickness, effective length and the permissible tolerances on each, for every class and size. Wall thickness rises with diameter — a small NP2 pipe has a far thinner wall than a 2200 mm one — and NP3 walls are generally thicker than NP2 walls at the same diameter. The exact values are as per IS 458 tables; a detailed spec sheet is available from us on request. On lengths, the commonly supplied effective lengths are 2.0 m and 2.5 m, though IS 458 permits longer lengths up to 4 m for larger-diameter collar-jointed pipes.

Joints

The standard covers three joint types, and the right one depends on the application:

Joint typeHow it worksWhere it is used
Spigot & socketPlain end fits into an enlarged socket end, sealed with a rubber ring (flexible joint)Required for pressure pipes; used for most non-pressure lines other than road culverts
Collar jointBoth pipe ends plain; a separate precast concrete collar covers the joint, with the gap filled with cement mortar or bitumen masticCommon on NP2 pipes for culverts and cross drains
Flush jointTongue on one end, groove on the other, giving a smooth internal surface; sealed with mortar, mastic or a rubber ringCommon on NP3 and NP4 pipes for culverts and cross drains

Workmanship and finish

Finally, IS 458 demands sound workmanship: dense, well-compacted concrete free of cracks and honeycombing, a smooth internal surface for good flow, and pipe ends square and true so joints seat properly. A pipe that fails on finish can be rejected even if its dimensions are correct.

The quality tests defined in IS 458

IS 458 backs its specifications with physical tests. Understanding what each test proves helps you read a spec sheet — and ask the right questions of any supplier.

Three-edge bearing test

This is the strength test. A pipe is laid on two bottom bearing strips and a load is applied along the crown (top line) of the pipe until it develops a crack of specified width, and then until failure. The load the pipe withstands, measured per metre of length, determines whether it truly meets its class — an NP3 pipe must carry a higher load than an NP2 pipe of the same diameter. The required load values are as per IS 458 tables. Per BIS practice, this test is carried out on sampled pipes from production lots rather than on every single pipe.

Hydrostatic test

This is the leak test. The pipe is filled with water and held at a specified internal pressure for a set time, and the surface is checked for leakage or sweating. It matters most for pressure (P class) pipes, which exist precisely to carry water under pressure, but it also verifies that non-pressure pipe walls are watertight.

Water absorption test

A sample cut from the pipe wall is dried, weighed, soaked in water and weighed again. The percentage of water it soaks up must stay below the limit in the standard. High absorption means porous concrete — which in the field translates to faster corrosion of reinforcement and a shorter pipe life.

Permeability test

Where absorption measures how much water the concrete soaks up, permeability measures whether water passes through the wall. Water is held against the pipe surface under a small head and the quantity seeping through is measured. A dense, well-cured, properly spun pipe shows negligible permeability; a poorly compacted one weeps.

How to use IS 458 when ordering pipes

You do not need to quote clause numbers in your purchase order. You need to state five things clearly, and IS 458 does the rest:

  1. Class — NP2, NP3 etc., based on the traffic and burial condition. If a design engineer or tender document has fixed the class, follow it exactly.
  2. Internal diameter — in mm (e.g. 600 mm, 900 mm), sized for the design flow.
  3. Joint type — spigot & socket with rubber ring for drainage or sewer lines; collar or flush joints are the norm for culverts and cross drains.
  4. Effective length — usually 2.0 m or 2.5 m; state it so quantities and transport are calculated correctly.
  5. Quantity and delivery point — transport is a real cost on concrete pipes, so mention the site location when asking for rates.

A well-written specification line looks like this: "RCC spun pipes as per IS 458:2021, class NP3, 900 mm internal dia, flush joint, 2.5 m effective length — 40 nos, delivered to site near Tiptur." One line, zero ambiguity.

Frequently asked questions

What is the current edition of IS 458?

The current edition is IS 458:2021, titled Precast Concrete Pipes (with and without reinforcement). It superseded IS 458:2003. If you are drafting a tender or purchase order today, refer to IS 458:2021.

Which IS 458 class should I choose for a road culvert?

It depends on the traffic above the pipe. NP2 (light-duty) suits cross drains and culverts under light traffic such as village and internal roads; NP3 (medium-duty) suits medium traffic; NP4 (heavy-duty) is specified for heavy traffic such as national highways. The project engineer or tender document normally fixes the class — follow it exactly.

Are BKN pipes BIS certified or ISI marked?

No. We manufacture as per IS 458 specifications but do not hold a BIS licence, so our pipes do not carry the ISI mark. We rely on strict in-house quality control — controlled batching, water curing, and dimensional and visual inspection — and we invite buyers to inspect our Kunigal and Tiptur units before ordering.

What diameters and classes does BKN supply under IS 458?

We manufacture NP2 and NP3 class RCC spun pipes: 300 mm to 1600 mm at Kunigal and up to 1200 mm at Tiptur, in 2.0 m and 2.5 m effective lengths. We do not manufacture NP4 or pressure (P class) pipes. Detailed spec sheets with wall thicknesses as per IS 458 tables are available on request.
